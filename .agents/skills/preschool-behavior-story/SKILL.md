# Preschool Behavior Story

Create a good read-aloud story first. Let the lesson live inside the plot rather
than explaining it to the child.

## Inputs

Collect or reasonably infer:

- the desired observable behavior;
- a recognizable real-world conflict where that behavior is difficult;
- optional child interests, setting, and personalization;
- parent style, defaulting to gentle-curious;
- length, defaulting to 12 spreads.

Translate abstract or negative lessons into safe, observable actions. For
example, interpret “do not waste food” as taking a small serving, asking for
more when hungry, and saving appropriate leftovers—not cleaning the plate or
eating past fullness. Ask only when a missing choice would materially change
the story.

## Design principle

Build the story around **Purpose → Affect → Agency**:

1. Give the protagonist or helper a concrete purpose a preschooler can grasp.
2. Create an emotional arc: anticipation, conflict, emotional dip, curiosity,
   small choice, success, and celebration.
3. Let the child character make the decisive behavioral choice.

The emotional dip may invite empathy but must not create guilt. Adults may
model, wonder, ask, encourage, inform, or help regulate emotion; they must not
command the central resolution or act as the moral narrator.

Use a familiar preschool conflict without portraying refusal, fear, anger,
disappointment, or mistakes as evidence that the child is bad.

Create one short, meaningful recurring phrase that is easy to say aloud and
usable outside the book. Use it about two to four times, not on every spread.

## Required workflow

Do not begin with manuscript prose. Read
[references/story-development.md](references/story-development.md), then work
through its six stages in order:

1. behavior brief;
2. story DNA;
3. spread outline;
4. critique and revision;
5. manuscript;
6. production handoff.

Show the intermediate artifacts unless the user requests only the manuscript.
Even when returning only a manuscript, perform the planning and critique before
writing it.

## Writing constraints

- Give every spread a visually distinct action or emotional beat.
- Use concrete nouns, active verbs, short sentences, dialogue, sound words,
  repetition, and read-aloud rhythm.
- Prefer curiosity, discovery, humor, empathy, and imaginative play over adult
  explanation.
- Make the child's first step small and achievable.
- Use realistic natural consequences; do not make scientifically misleading
  promises.
- Keep storytelling separate from rendering style and image-generation
  instructions.

Never use shame, humiliation, threats, fear as coercion, withdrawal of
affection, exaggerated punishment, manipulative sadness, moral labeling, or
obedience-as-virtue.

For eating stories, also respect fullness, permit saved leftovers, avoid plate
cleaning, avoid immediate health or strength promises, and do not label foods
as morally good or bad.

## Quality bar

Revise until all three tests pass:

- **Child:** Is there a reason to care what happens next?
- **Parent:** Would a caregiver willingly read it repeatedly?
- **Transfer:** Does it leave behind a simple behavior or phrase that can recur
  naturally in daily life?

Educational usefulness alone is insufficient if the story is not enjoyable.
